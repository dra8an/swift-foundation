# How the Islamic crescent calendars work

A guide to the Islamic calendars whose months start when the new crescent can be seen. The first part is the same for all of them. The table after it gives what each authority asks. The civil, tabular and Umm al-Qura table calendars are in `Fixed_Month_Calendars.md`.

## What all of them do

Think of the moon as a clock in the sky that everyone can see. One full turn of that clock is a month.

**The moon plays hide and seek.** Once a month the moon passes right next to the sun and disappears in its glare. That moment is the new moon, and nobody can see it. About a day later the moon has moved a little away from the sun, and a thin crescent shows up just after sunset. The new month begins when that crescent can be seen.

**These authorities do not go outside and look. They calculate.** On the evening of day 29 of the month, each one asks a yes-or-no question about the moon at the moment the sun sets.

- If the answer is yes, tomorrow is day 1 of the new month.
- If not, the old month gets a 30th day, and the new month starts the day after.

So every month has 29 or 30 days, never anything else.

**Because it is all calculation, the calendar is known ahead of time.** Each authority computes its months in advance and publishes them.

## The criteria

The authorities differ in where they look and what they ask.

| Authority | Where it looks | What it asks at sunset |
|---|---|---|
| Türkiye, Diyanet | Anywhere on Earth, before Greenwich midnight | Moon at least 8° from the sun and 5° high |
| Saudi Arabia, Umm al-Qura | One spot: the Kaaba in Mecca | Has the new moon already happened, and does the moon set after the sun |
| Malaysia | One spot: its westernmost point | Moon at least 3° high and 6.4° from the sun |
| Indonesia | Its far northwest, around Banda Aceh | Same two numbers as Malaysia |
| Singapore | One spot: Tuas | Same two numbers as Malaysia |

Three differences are worth knowing.

- **Only Diyanet needs a deadline.** It searches the whole globe, so it must say when the search stops. That is the Greenwich midnight. The others each stand in one fixed place, so their own sunset is the only moment that matters.
- **Saudi Arabia does not ask whether anyone could see the crescent.** It only asks whether the moon is new and still above the horizon when the sun goes down. A moon that sets one minute after the sun counts, though no human eye could find it.
- **Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore share one rule.** They agreed it together, and it is easier to meet than Diyanet's. They also judge only from their own region, so a crescent visible in America does not help them.

**All of them changed their rule over time.** Diyanet changed its deadline. The Southeast Asian countries used an easier rule until 2021. Saudi Arabia has had at least three rules since the 1970s. Each calendar was computed with the rule of its day.

## Türkiye (Diyanet)

**Two questions.** Diyanet asks two questions about the moon at the moment the sun sets somewhere:

1. Has the moon moved far enough from the sun? It must be at least 8 degrees away, which is about sixteen moon-widths.
2. Is the moon high enough? It must be at least 5 degrees above the horizon, about three fingers held at arm's length.

If both answers are yes, the crescent counts as visible there.

**It does not matter where.** Sunset travels around the Earth like a line sweeping westward. Diyanet follows that line and finds the first place and moment where both answers become yes. One place on Earth is enough for the whole world.

**There is a deadline.** The deadline is midnight in Greenwich, England. The crescent must become visible somewhere before that midnight for tomorrow to be day 1.

**One extra rule since 2017.** Sunset reaches America after Greenwich midnight, so America kept missing the deadline by a few hours. Now a late crescent still counts if two things hold. It must be visible on American land, not only over the ocean. And the new moon must have happened before dawn in New Zealand, so that nobody on Earth starts the month before the moon is even new.

**Diyanet works years ahead.** It computes whole calendar years in one go and publishes them. The calendar on the wall for 2030 already exists.

**The rule changed over the years.** The two questions never changed. What changed is the deadline rule. Before 1980 Diyanet used a different, older test. From 1980 it used the strict Greenwich midnight. For a few years after 2009 it was more forgiving, then strict again, and from 2017 it has the America rule. Each batch of years was computed once, with the rule and the program of its day, and never recomputed. That is why one formula cannot cover everything, and why a handful of months sit on the wrong side of a line by minutes.

## How the whole Earth is checked

Nobody watches from every place on Earth. It is all calculation, and the whole Earth comes down to one line.

**The ingredients.** Published formulas give the position of the sun and the moon at any moment, to within a minute. They are the formulas that predict eclipses years ahead. With the latitude and longitude of a place, geometry gives what someone standing there would see.

**Three facts shrink the problem.**

1. **The moon's distance from the sun is the same for everyone.** It does not depend on where you stand. It grows by about half a degree every hour, so the 8 degrees are reached at one moment for the whole planet.
2. **Sunset is a line, not an area.** At any instant the sun is setting only along one curve from north to south. That curve sweeps westward around the globe once a day.
3. **Later is better.** The moon keeps pulling away from the sun, so a place where the sun sets later sees a higher crescent.

**So one line is enough.** The best chance before the deadline is where the sun sets exactly at Greenwich midnight. The calculation walks down that curve in small steps of latitude and works out how high the moon stands at each point. One point that is high enough starts the month.

**The America rule works the same way.** On each latitude the westernmost point of the mainland has the latest sunset. So only the west coast is checked, from Alaska to Chile, and nothing inland.

**Two small corrections at each point.** The formulas give the view from the center of the Earth. Someone on the surface sees the moon up to a degree lower. Air bends light and lifts the moon a little.

## The sunset line is tilted

The line leans because the Earth's axis is tilted, and the lean changes with the season.

| Season | The sunset line |
|---|---|
| March and September | Runs almost straight from north to south |
| Northern summer | Leans, with its northern end pointing east |
| Northern winter | Leans the other way |

The lean is strongest in June and December, about 23 degrees, which is the tilt of the Earth's axis. In northern summer the line also stops short of the pole, because inside the Arctic Circle the sun does not set.

This is why Alaska decides so many close months. In May the line can pass near the date line at 30° north and through mainland Alaska at 60° north at the same moment. The far north is still catching a sunset that the tropics finished long before.

The calculation needs no special case for the lean. For each latitude it finds where on that latitude the sun is setting, and the tilted line follows from that.
